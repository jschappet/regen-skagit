##   A Farewell to Childs Creek Farm

_Reflections with Cindy, as she prepares to say goodbye to Childs Creek Farm after forty years_

The farm sits halfway between the foothills of the Cascade Mountains and the wide, open lowlands of the Skagit Valley. This location creates the conditions for frequent rainbows.

Cindy remembers that over and over, as she drove home to the farm, there seemed to be a rainbow ahead of her, guiding her home.

![Rainbow](/assets/images/uploads/0a2d6345-4678-4ccf-9c17-1615e989c82b.jpeg)

For forty years, Cindy has tended Childs Creek Farm, watched seasons turn, planted trees, built gardens, raised children, welcomed young people, experimented with solar energy, restored wetlands, and paid close attention to what was happening around her.

![Mailbox and Sign](/assets/images/uploads//9bca864a-fc39-486e-9238-a728aec3f2d8.jpeg)

For 40 years, this land held her tender human body. 

![Cindy](/assets/images/uploads/447f91cd-acc1-41a3-8c15-d6d0063c976b.jpeg)

There were so many relationships: sun, soil, new sprouts, old plant friends, honey bees, dragonflies, butterflies (pollinator friends). She brought her children and many friends to the gardens to pick fruit, vegetables and a variety of herbs year after year. Sunrises, friends, the loss of her husband and daughter—the business of living happened here, among the plants and paths and the wandering creek and many trees.

Now, in September 2026, she is passing the property into new hands.

We walked the farm with her one more time to listen to what she has learned from a place she has spent so long observing.

## A farm is more than what you plant

We began our walk in the old orchard near the western edge of the property.

![Cindy and Schappet looking at the Strawberry Trees](/assets/images/uploads/457434f3-289c-43c7-ad1b-0f0a97c98f6d.jpeg)

Cindy pointed out a hedgerow: blackberries alongside Oregon grape and red-flowering currant. Above it, strawberry trees and other deciduous trees have grown over the years. At the southern corner, Douglas fir and Western Red Cedar reach toward the sky.

The berm itself began with fill dirt left over from building their house. They shaped the extra soil along the property boundary and then planted it.

That simple decision has become a living edge. Trees, shrubs, birds, shade and wildlife grow thickly, creating a private, sheltered boundary around the farm.

She told us a story about her friend bear. For nearly a decade, the bear came every autumn to the old Gravenstein apple tree at the corner of the orchard, the farm's oldest apple tree. It would climb into the branches, scrape at the bark, and shake the apples loose, leaving piles of evidence in the fall grass.

![Apple Tree](/assets/images/uploads/9f058a5b-3044-44d8-9422-3eb3b9a30619.jpeg)

It wasn't the only animal who enjoyed the apples. Elk grazed the drops. The neighbor's horses got a bite too.

It's been about two years since Cindy last saw the bear. She said it the way you'd mention an old neighbor who moved on. 

## Letting things grow

Cindy kept returning to one word:

**Diversity.**

It is in the orchard, where heirloom apples survive alongside newer varieties. Here asparagus grows beside rhubarb, herbs beside flowers, and kale beside beets. Companion planting occurs throughout the garden. 

The plants naturally propagate to expand the diversity with a mind of their own.

![Community Garden](/assets/images/uploads/d042f940-b60c-4c75-88be-0380a03a89ac.jpeg)

The perennial and pollinator gardens continue even though Cindy doesn't have the energy to maintain them as she once did.

“It just does its thing,” she said.

As we passed the old goat shed Cindy cataloged the animals that contributed to Childs Creek. The family kept sheep, ducks, chickens, pigs, rabbits and goats and an assortment of friendly cats that kept the mice at bay. 

## The wetland remembers

Down below the upper part of the property, the land changes.

When Cindy first arrived, the wetland was filled with skunk cabbage and the three seasonal fields were being encroached by blackberries.

Around the turn of the century, the wetland became part of the first Conservation Reserve Enhancement Project in Skagit County. Volunteers from the Fishery Enhancement group and the Service, Knowledge, Youth program (SKY), along with friends and family worked together to plant eight hundred trees and shrubs representing twenty-two species.

Twenty-five years later, Cindy estimates that those original plantings have become something closer to 8,000 plants with greater diversity than before.

Now birds move through the forest. Shade has changed what can grow beneath the canopy. The water moves differently and the Coho salmon are returning to Childs creek.

Many hands and many seasons brought the forest back to the river’s edge.

## A home built from what might have been lost

Cindy's home carries another part of the story.

![Ceder House](/assets/images/uploads/62ed8c94-17d7-466c-938d-922a2cc559a1.jpeg)

The cedar house was rescued and reconstructed rather than discarded. It was then adapted using a south-facing passive-solar design. Cindy paid attention to where the sun came from and how warmth could be held.

![Sun Solar House](/assets/images/uploads/6fd9e76f-06d5-4ba4-b12b-c1cb6d582207.jpeg)

In 2007, she moved from the property's original small cottage into the restored house.

## The abundance that asks for nothing back

As we walked, the farm kept offering us something to eat.

Plums, grapes, pears, and apples line the path, and hardy kiwis hang above the rustic shed.

![Hardy Kiwis](/assets/images/uploads/cabae5c5-5bfa-41fb-8453-ad61767ed878.jpeg)

![Grapes on the vine.](/assets/images/uploads/62f604a0-09ac-4e0d-9688-7dcc1c6768f5.jpeg)

Each step held something delicious for anyone willing to stop and taste.

Cindy's favorite apples are the Sweet Williams and Williams Pride. Her favorite plums are the golden-yellow Green Gages, especially when they are very ripe.

And then there is the lemon balm, Melissa Officianalis. The lemon balm grows thick alongside oregano, sage, fennel and peppermint.

For years, Cindy has made a restorative tea from lemon balm and mint, sometimes adding spring nettles for extra minerals.

She doesn't want to keep the recipe to herself.

“There's no reason to trademark it now,” she laughed.

## A farm for people, too

Child’s Creek Farm was never only about plants.

There is a community building on the property that began when Cindy's daughters were teenagers. What started as additional space for her growing family became somewhere to gather, make things, retreat, create and dream.

![Community House](/assets/images/uploads/71e763cd-6726-422d-8334-cf7a8315c849.jpeg)

The garden next door is called the Community Garden.

During the COVID years, the farm became home to young people participating as WWOOFers and interns. The community building and the community garden were inhabited. About a dozen WWOOFers came through, volunteering anywhere from two weeks to two months.

It was a time of great joy and play. Together, they worked on the food forest, pulled blackberry roots, competed to remove the biggest ones, and even made art sculptures from the roots.

Cindy remembers those years fondly as new relationships became the silver lining of the pandemic.

## What does a farm look like 150 years from now?

At one point, we asked Cindy what she would like to see in the future.

She hoped for large trees. Many kinds of plants and animals. Places where people could meet, create, walk, enjoy one another and dream.

As she pondered, she noticed something about the way she has spent these decades: giving the land possibilities and trusting what would happen from there.

She hopes the new stewards will honor the farm in the same way, so that the relationships between tree, wetland, spider, shade, bear, thimbleberry, pear and kiwi can continue to deepen and intertwine.

## Saying goodbye

When we asked Cindy what would be hardest to leave behind, it wasn't one particular tree or garden.

She said she would keep her memories.

What she would miss was simply being here: walking around, observing nature, experiencing the peace, and seeing the extraordinary variety that had developed over time.

## Passing it on

Cindy is selling Childs Creek Farm to younger owners who are teachers.

She hopes they will love it.

She hopes they will enjoy the sun and wind, the birds and animals, the gardens and trees.

And she hopes that their love for one another, as a family, will deepen as they care for the farm.

She would like them to stay curious about what is already here.

To notice what wants to grow.

To enjoy what they find.

And, eventually, to pass something forward themselves.

### A blessing

Cindy's hope for the farm is simple:

**May you continue to be loved.**

**May you continue to be diverse.**

**May people continue to gather here.**

**May the trees grow tall.**

**May the birds find their way through.**

**May the water keep moving.**

**May those who come after us learn to listen.**
