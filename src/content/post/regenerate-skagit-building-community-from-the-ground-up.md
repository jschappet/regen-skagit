*Exploring a local network of people, relationships, and small experiments that help us learn how to work together.*

At Regenerate Skagit, we're exploring how to build a local system that makes it easier for people to find each other, pursue shared interests, experiment with different ways of living, and learn how to work together.

We're not just trying to start a group, organize events, or create a local social network.

The important part is that **the system itself is something we want to experiment with and refine.**

We don't want to design a whole community in advance and then recruit people to participate in it. We want to create a structure that helps people build community from the ground up.

This is a working framework. It identifies the parts we're interested in developing, the questions we want to explore, and what we can start doing now.

## A Network of People, Experiments, and Relationships

We imagine several interconnected parts supporting one another.

### A Growing Network of People

People who are interested in learning, experimenting, and making things happen locally.

They don't all need the same worldview or the same level of awareness. They need a way to participate that works for them.

### Small, Interest-Based Experiments

Groups form around something people actually want to try: cooking together, learning farm work, testing a neighborhood food distribution point, painting a room, preparing for floods, or organizing a neighborhood potluck.

### A Repeatable Method

A simple structure for going from interest to learning, action, reflection, and a decision about what comes next.

This is something we're developing and testing.

### Connection and Relational Skills

A place to practice being in relationship, listening, expressing needs, working through differences, and repairing conflict.

This helps people do more than complete projects together.

### Local Infrastructure

Shared knowledge about gathering spaces, local projects, people with skills, available resources, and ways to contact and organize with neighbors.

### Shared Learning and Memory

A record of experiments, what people learned, what they tried, what worked, and what they want to try next.

This lets knowledge accumulate instead of disappearing when an experiment ends.

These are not six separate organizations we need to build. They are six functions that the larger system needs to support.

At the beginning, one person and a simple document could cover several of them.

## The Regenerate Experiment Cycle

We want a structure that supports people's excitement without making them commit to a long-term organization, follow a leader's agenda, or produce a predetermined outcome.

We're beginning with a simple cycle.

### 1. Find the Interest

What are you excited about? What would you like to learn, change, make, or try?

### 2. Find Other Interested People

Who wants to explore something similar? What common interest is enough to start?

### 3. Explore the Question

What do we need to learn? What is already known? What resources, skills, or relationships would help?

### 4. Choose a Small Experiment

What could we actually try? Who wants to participate? What is the smallest useful action?

### 5. Do the Experiment

Try something in the real world. Keep the scale appropriate to the group's time, energy, and resources.

### 6. Share What Happened

What did we learn? What surprised us? What worked or didn't? What changed in our understanding?

### 7. Decide What Comes Next

Continue, change the approach, try something new, pause, or dissolve the group. People can join another experiment.

**The method should be a scaffold, not a rigid sequence.**

Some groups might need two weeks. Others might need six weeks. Some experiments might take an afternoon.

We're interested in exploring a group that can renew itself every two weeks, helping people explore different interests without feeling trapped in a commitment.

Two things are worth separating:

- **The experiment's duration:** How long it takes to try something.
- **The group's membership cycle:** When people can join, leave, or reconsider their participation.

Those don't have to be the same. A group might take six weeks to complete an experiment while allowing new participants to join at a designated check-in.

### A Simple Two-Week Prototype

**Week 1: Interest and Exploration**

Each person shares their interest, what they want to learn, and an experiment they might try.

The group helps each other clarify questions and identify useful next steps.

**Week 2: Action and Reflection**

People share what they tried, what they learned, and what they want to do next.

The group decides whether to continue together, change direction, or finish.

People shouldn't have to produce a polished report every week. A paragraph, a few notes, a demonstration, or a conversation could be enough.

The documentation should support the experiment rather than become another obligation.

## Questions We're Exploring

There are several important design questions. These aren't reasons to delay starting. They are things to investigate as we test the method.

### How Does an Individual Interest Become a Shared Experiment?

One person wants to learn to paint. Another wants to learn about flood preparation. A third wants to organize a community meal.

The network needs to make it easy for each person to say:

- This is what interests me.
- This is what I want to try.
- This is what I need help with.
- This is the kind of participation I am looking for.

But it also needs a way to help people discover overlaps.

Maybe three people don't share an identical goal, but they all want to learn practical skills and spend time working alongside others.

The method needs to help people discover common ground without requiring them to have identical interests.

### How Do People Get from Conversation to Action?

A conversation can contain exciting ideas without producing a structure that makes anything happen.

Our experiment groups need some light scaffolding:

- A clearly stated interest or question.
- A person willing to convene the group.
- A next action that is small enough to complete.
- A way to ask for help.
- A date to reconnect.
- A decision about what to do with the learning.

The convenor doesn't need to be the expert or permanent leader. Their role is to help the group maintain momentum and make its next steps visible.

### What Happens When People Disagree or Hurt Each Other?

We see mediation and conflict repair as part of the system, not an optional extra.

There are three related areas:

**Everyday communication:** Listening, asking questions, clarifying assumptions, and expressing needs.

**Group process:** Making decisions, distributing work, addressing unequal participation, and handling disagreement.

**Conflict repair:** Responding when trust has been damaged and determining whether and how a relationship can continue.

These require different skills.

A connection gathering can help people practice the first. Experiment groups provide opportunities to practice the second. The third requires a more deliberate process and people who know how to facilitate it.

We don't need a complete mediation system before inviting ten people to experiment together.

We do need clear expectations about respectful participation, a way to raise concerns, and a plan for getting help when a conflict exceeds the group's capacity.

### How Does Knowledge Accumulate?

We want a fabric of experiments, not a collection of disconnected events.

That means the network should eventually record more than what happened. It should help people find:

- What others have already tried.
- Who has relevant experience.
- Which resources or gathering places are available.
- What questions remain unanswered.
- What could be replicated or adapted elsewhere.

A simple shared experiment record could include the original interest, participants, actions taken, lessons learned, and possible next steps.

Over time, these records become local knowledge that other people can build on.

### How Does Participation Stay Voluntary?

Our bottom-up approach depends on people being able to follow their interests without being pressured into someone else's vision.

That creates some practical questions:

- Can someone participate without agreeing with every part of Regenerate Skagit?
- Can they leave without being treated as a failure?
- How do groups handle commitments when someone loses interest?
- What happens when a person wants to contribute but has very little time?
- How are decisions made when the group cannot agree?

These are not peripheral issues.

They determine whether the system actually supports self-leadership or quietly recreates the pressure of conventional organizations.

## Values That Shape the Structure

Rather than beginning with a long list of values and trying to enforce them, we're interested in identifying the values that already matter to us and translating each one into a practical design choice.

### Interest-Led Participation

People choose what they want to explore rather than being assigned a role in someone else's project.

**In practice:** Open calls for interests, optional participation, and easy ways to form new groups.

### Self-Leadership

People have room to initiate projects, make decisions, and take responsibility for their own participation.

**In practice:** Participants can convene experiments, propose actions, and decide how much responsibility to take on.

### Relationships Before Outcomes

The quality of how people work together matters alongside what they accomplish.

**In practice:** Time for connection, reflection, and addressing difficulties is part of the process.

### Learning Through Experimentation

People don't need to know the answer before they begin.

**In practice:** Small trials, honest reporting, and permission to change direction.

### Local Reciprocity

People build relationships around the needs, resources, knowledge, and capacities of the place where they live.

**In practice:** Local project records, skill-sharing, resource exchanges, and connections between households, farms, and community spaces.

### Accountability Without Coercion

People can make real commitments without relying on guilt, blame, or forced conformity.

**In practice:** Explicit agreements, honest check-ins, renegotiation when circumstances change, and repair when commitments affect others.

These are starting hypotheses.

We can test whether the structures actually produce the behavior we want.

**A value is not fully implemented just because it appears on a website.**

## How We Begin Building the Network

We're interested in developing three things in parallel, keeping each one small.

### Start the Experiments

One possibility is approaching six existing groups to test the method with people who already gather together.

We could ask permission to introduce the idea or facilitate a short activity.

The goal is not necessarily to recruit six groups into Regenerate Skagit. It is to learn:

- Which interests people are already motivated to act on.
- What prevents them from acting together.
- What kind of support they would actually use.
- Whether a structured experiment group sounds appealing.
- What would make participation easier.

We can also ask people individually, through ordinary conversation.

We don't have to introduce the whole vision every time.

Questions like these could open the conversation:

- What have you been wanting to do that you haven't gotten around to?
- Is there anything you wish you had other people to learn or work on with?
- What would you be excited to try over the next couple of weeks?
- What makes it hard to get people involved in things around here?

### Establish a Small Home for the Work

We don't need a local Facebook replacement to start.

At the beginning, we need somewhere to publish the basic idea, collect interests, record experiments, and communicate with participants.

A simple website and a shared document or group space could do this.

Later, we can test whether a dedicated local online community adds enough value to justify the work of operating it.

There is an important distinction between two ideas:

**Regenerate Skagit:** A network of people, experiments, relationships, and shared learning.

**SkagitNet:** A possible digital infrastructure for the wider local community.

They could eventually support each other, but they don't need to launch together.

A full local network would raise additional questions about verification of local membership, privacy, moderation, governance, accessibility, and sustainable operating costs.

Those are real design problems. We can postpone them until we know what people need the platform to do.

### Start Publishing What We Learn

We want to create a record of our developing methodology, including what we learn from actual experiments.

We don't need to wait until the system is fully defined.

In fact, publishing the questions and lessons as we work through them could help us find people who are interested in the process itself.

Some questions worth exploring:

- Why start with interests instead of organizations?
- How do people turn an idea into an experiment?
- What helps people follow through together?
- What makes it easier to get involved locally?
- How do we build trust and handle conflict in groups?
- What can a two-week experiment teach us?
- What infrastructure would make local cooperation easier?

We want to keep the distinction clear between what we believe, what we're testing, and what we've learned.

That will make the project more useful to others and give us a record of how the methodology develops.

## Starting Small

Our first version could be as simple as:

**Ten people. Two experiments. One repeatable method. One shared record of what we learn.**

We don't need a finished set of values, a mediation training program, a digital platform, or a plan for 2,000 people.

And we don't want to measure success only by whether people completed their projects.

We also want to ask:

- Did people find it easier to participate?
- Did they learn something useful?
- Did they form a new relationship?
- Did they become more able to initiate their own experiments?

Those are indicators that the underlying system is working.

## The Longer-Term Vision: A Local Network That Supports Many Forms of Gathering

This approach could eventually support much more than project groups.

People might use the system to create:

- A neighborhood email list where members personally know one another.
- A women's group or men's group with its own gathering agreements.
- A local food resilience network.
- A recurring community meal.
- A skill-sharing or coworking group.
- A group for learning mediation and conflict repair.
- A network of gathering spaces with clear information about availability, costs, and hosting requirements.
- A local directory of businesses, projects, resources, and community conversations.

The shared foundation would be a repeatable way to organize people around an interest, create workable agreements, take action, and learn together.

Different groups could add their own structures where needed.

We want to avoid trying to design every possible application before the foundation has been tested.

The same basic method might work well for a painting group and need substantial adaptation for a mediation group.

That is something our experiments can reveal.

And the vision of a network connecting 2,000 people doesn't mean Regenerate Skagit needs to personally organize 2,000 people.

It could mean developing a method that other people learn, adapt, and use to convene their own groups, with the wider network connecting their work.

**That is a different scaling model: developing a replicable social process, not just growing a single organization.**

## Building Community and Building Culture

There are two related but different ambitions within this work.

The first is to help people get things done together by making it easier to organize around their interests.

The second is to help people develop a different culture by changing how they relate, learn, make decisions, and act together.

The first can attract people who simply want help painting a room, starting a neighborhood potluck, or finding farm work.

The second is the deeper cultural experiment we're interested in.

People don't necessarily need to agree with that second ambition to participate in the first.

**The method itself can embody the values, while the culture develops through experience.**

We want to explore what happens when people come together around something they genuinely care about, have the freedom to participate in ways that work for them, and learn through shared experience.

Not by designing a community and asking people to fit into it.

But by creating the conditions in which community can emerge.

## An Invitation

We're still developing this approach. We have questions, ideas, and hypotheses, but we're not starting with all the answers.

We want to learn by doing, alongside people who are interested in trying things together.

So we'll leave you with a question:

**When you join with others to accomplish something practical, what would you most want to experience about how people work together that is different from what you're used to?**

That question may be the bridge between our practical experiments and our larger vision for a regenerative culture here in Skagit Valley.

And perhaps the best way to discover the answer is to try something together.
