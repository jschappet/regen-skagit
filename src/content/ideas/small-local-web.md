## Idea

A **Small Local Web** is a network of simple websites owned and run by people, farms, projects, and organizations in the Skagit watershed. Instead of relying on distant platforms and algorithms, neighbors publish directly for neighbors—sharing events, needs, tools, knowledge, and stories in ways that are easy to find and link together. Over time, these small sites weave into a resilient local information ecosystem that reflects the real relationships and rhythms of the valley.

## Why It Matters

A **Small Local Web** strengthens the bioregional culture Regenerate Skagit is working to grow. When neighbors can easily publish and connect their own small websites, local knowledge stays close to the land and the people who live with it. Farmers, artists, watershed stewards, educators, and community groups can share events, needs, tools, and stories without being filtered or buried by distant platforms and algorithms.

This kind of web builds digital soil for the region: information that is easier to discover, link, and preserve across the watershed. Instead of scattered posts that disappear in feeds, communities gain a living network of locally rooted sites that reflect real relationships and seasonal rhythms in the valley.

Over time, a Small Local Web helps Skagit become more resilient and self-reliant. It makes it easier for people to find each other, collaborate on regenerative projects, and participate in the shared work of caring for land, water, and community.

## What Could Happen Next

People in the valley begin spinning up small, simple websites—farms posting their harvest schedules, artists sharing upcoming shows, watershed groups documenting restoration work, and neighbors announcing gatherings. Each site links to a few others nearby, slowly forming a visible map of relationships across the Skagit watershed.

A shared directory or index emerges that helps people discover these sites without centralizing them. Instead of one platform controlling everything, the network grows through links, tags, and community-maintained lists.

Local events, needs, and opportunities start traveling through this web. Someone looking for volunteers for a creek restoration can find the right groups. A farmer can announce extra produce. A tool library can share what is available. The information moves through the network the way nutrients move through soil—locally, repeatedly, and in many directions.

Over time, lightweight tools and guides appear to help more people participate: simple site templates, workshops on “how to make a small website,” and gatherings where neighbors help each other publish their first pages.

As the Small Local Web grows, it becomes a living memory of the valley—stories, projects, and knowledge accumulating across many locally owned sites. Instead of disappearing in social media feeds, the work of the community becomes easier to find, link, and build upon year after year.
